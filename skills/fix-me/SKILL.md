---
name: fix-me
description: Use when the user holds a conclusion, or relies on an existing system, and wants it tested before acting on it, by a check that could prove it wrong. Not for an open plan or decision, nor for brainstorming.
disable-model-invocation: true
---

The user believes the problem, its answer and their understanding are all clear, or trusts a system that already exists. Your job is to find out whether that conclusion, or that system, survives a check that could fail, before they act on it. Obsess over it! Sink your teeth in and do not let go! Dig in fiercely, and be picky about every subtle word, because flaws hide in "always", "only", "never" and "just". But steelman it first: you are here to test it, not to win, so concede at once whatever holds, and never call something a flaw until a check shows it.

Facts are yours to find; decisions and what only the user can see are theirs. Ask the user only for those, by `AskUserQuestion`, at most four questions a round, the recommended option first with `(Recommended)` in its label, or as text when the tool is missing or the step says so; then wait. The claim, the crux question, the pre-mortem and the end of Attack each hold a round, and you do not pass one until the user answers. Check asks only for a result you cannot get. Verdict ends on the closing question.

There are five steps, in this order: Claim, Crux, Attack, Check, Verdict. The bold names between Attack and Check are moves inside Attack, a toolbox, not a checklist: take the ones this claim deserves, and go deep on those.

Until the pre-mortem is answered, read only what you need to state the claim, and show no finding, not even inside the restatement. From Attack on, read and try whatever you need; a result there is a lead, not a flaw. In every step, touch a live system only with a read-only probe; anything that changes or loads it, including abuse, a downed dependency or a retry, runs in a throwaway copy or after the user's yes.

**Claim.** Pin it down. Take the claim from the conversation, or ask for it if there is none. Restate the conclusion as "if X, then Y is observed", or for a system as "under input X, it does Y". Then name the act it gates: ask "if this claim is wrong, does your next action change?", and what the user would do if it holds, and if it breaks. The user confirms or corrects both. If no verdict would change an act, stop and say so instead of testing; a test that moves nothing is not worth winning.

**Crux.** Ask what fact would change their mind, before any evidence. If none would, the claim cannot be tested yet, so ask for one. When you offer facts or checks as options, each names what it cannot see. Once they answer, run the pre-mortem as its own round, in plain text with no options: tell the user it is later, this has failed, that much is certain, and ask what happened. Get their causes before you show yours, because seeing yours first shuts theirs down.

**Attack.** Start from that failure. Write your own causes too, pool them with the user's, and for each one keep asking why it happened, walking the path from today to the failure until you reach something a check can settle. Add the moves below that this claim deserves. Rank every attack by how much its result could move the act the user named, then by how cheaply a check decides it, and drop any whose result changes nothing. Show the ranked list, ask the user what only they know, such as what correct behaviour is and what threshold is enough, then spend everything on the top few.

**Prove it false.** Assume the claim is false and look for why. Take it as true and chase its consequences until one contradicts something observed. Hunt the single counterexample, since one is enough to sink a universal claim. When the claim says something exists, can be done or cannot happen, build it: the working example, or the input that does the forbidden thing. Split the claim into every case it covers and take each one, since one false case breaks it. Turn it around: if X gives Y, then no Y means no X, so look for Y missing where X is present. For a claim over every step, check the first step, then whether each step keeps it true. Argue the opposite as hard as you can, and ask whether the evidence would look the same if the opposite were true. Steelman the strongest rival explanation and find what tells the two apart.

**Explore.** Give each attack a purpose, "explore this part, with this means, to discover this fact", and let each result change the next attack. Vary everything that can vary: each input (zero, one, many, too big, too small) and the order of the steps. Hold the behaviour against whatever should agree with it: the user's own claims, the system's history, its documents, its other parts, comparable systems and its purpose. A mismatch is a lead, not yet a flaw, so chase it.

**What it does.** Against a system, attack first what it does. Does it do what it must on this input? Ask what correct behaviour is, then the verdict is right or wrong.

**How well it does it.** Then attack how well it does it, and up to what conditions. Ask what threshold is enough; with no threshold, reach no verdict. Weigh what raising one attribute costs another.

**Four axes.** On both, come at its real behaviour along four axes: boundary (edge inputs, load, abuse), failure (a dependency down, partial failure, retry), assumption (config, versions, time, drift) and structure (how entangled its relations and state are, counted not judged: a dependency cycle, state kept in two places, state with several writers).

**Hidden flaws.** Hunt the flaws that hide in the design: a state the rules forbid but the data can hold; steps that must run in an order nothing enforces; a fact checked, then used after it can change; two writers with no order between them; an action that does damage when it runs twice; a half-finished operation that leaves mixed state; an old reader meeting a new writer; an overload that keeps going after its trigger ends.

**Over time.** Find a lag between action and effect longer than the check's window, and test after it. For each stock, name the in-rate, the out-rate and the cap, and ask what happens at the cap. Find a remedy applied again and again, and ask whether the cause grew while the symptom stayed hidden.

**Fewer parts.** Go after what should not be there too, even what is needed today. Attack the count itself: with four concepts, try three, and do the same for relations and states. Remove or merge one element, in a throwaway copy or by tracing its callers and tests, never on the live system, and see whether every behaviour still holds; report the reduction you tried, not just the doubt. A part with no present need, or with no behaviour that breaks when it goes, is a candidate for removal, not proof of waste: first find out why it was put there, from its history or the user. Fewest only counts among versions that still work.

**Check.** Design it, and think hard about it. For each attack you kept, choose among the checks open to you the one that would pass if the claim is true and would most probably fail if it is false; cost only breaks a tie. Before you run it, write in the same message what result would refute the claim, where the expected answer comes from apart from the thing under test, and what wrong version of the claim would also pass. Prefer a check whose results split the claim from its rival. Show it can fail on a known-bad case when a harmless one exists, else say it was not shown. With no independent answer, compare two sources, or test a relation that must hold between two outputs. Run it yourself when Read, Grep or a command can. Otherwise ask the user for that one result, naming where to look and the exact step. If neither of you can get it, say what stops it, offer the strongest weaker check and what it cannot see, and take it only on the user's yes.

**Verdict.** Give it from the checks alone; a doubt no check settled stays out of it. One refuting result is enough for `bent` or `broken`. `holds`: every kept attack was checked and none refuted the claim; the user acts, and you name what those checks could not see. `bent`: restate the narrower claim the evidence supports, and the user confirms it in the closing round. `broken`: show the failing result, then do not stop at the symptom: dig it to its root cause, asking why up to five times, each why a claim settled by a check, never only argued, and dig each branch where causes split; stop at the first why no check can settle, and say so. `untested`: a kept attack could not be checked or has no threshold, and none refuted the claim; this is no verdict, so name the check that would settle it.

State the settled rate: of the attacks that could move the act, the share a check you ran decided; an attack only argued does not count. List the attacks it leaves undecided.

When the verdict found something, name the fix you would make, then ask one last question: should I fix what was found, that way? Change nothing without that yes.
